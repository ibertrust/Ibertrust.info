Apostille and sworn translation for a Spanish NIF: when they are required, when they can be waived or simplified

2026-11-24 · IberTrust Global

The general rule published by the AEAT is straightforward. If the documents filed with a Spanish NIF application were not issued in Spain, they must be translated into Spanish and shown to be authentic. In practice that usually means an apostille (or consular legalisation if the country is not a party to the Hague Apostille Convention) and, where required, an official translation.

The same rule is not applied in every file in the same way. Some exemptions and simplifications are official and general; others come from a written AEAT information reply that can apply to certain online sellers. This guide sets out where an apostille is obtained, when it can be waived or simplified, and when a sworn translation can be replaced by an unofficial one. It complements our earlier notes on NIF without apostille for online sellers and on unofficial translation of English, French or Italian documents.

Where the apostille is issued

An apostille is issued in the country where the public document originates, by the competent authority designated under Article 6 of the Hague Convention of 5 October 1961. It is not issued by the AEAT, and it is not obtained in Spain for a foreign document. The official list of competent authorities is published by the Hague Conference on Private International Law (HCCH).

  • United States. State documents (for example a certificate issued by a state company registry) are apostilled by that state’s Secretary of State. Federal documents are apostilled by the U.S. Department of State.
  • United Kingdom. The competent authority is the FCDO Legalisation Office.
  • China. For documents issued in mainland China, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs is the competent authority. Foreign Affairs Offices of provinces, autonomous regions, municipalities and certain cities, authorised by the Ministry, may issue an apostille on public documents issued within their jurisdiction. The Convention entered into force for China on 7 November 2023. Apostilles issued in mainland China can be verified at the consular website of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. Hong Kong and Macao have their own designated authorities.

If the country where the document was issued is not a party to the Convention, an apostille cannot be used. The document must go through diplomatic or consular legalisation, as described by the AEAT and by the Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs.

When an apostille can be waived — and when it cannot

EU Regulation 2016/1191 exempts certain public documents issued by one Member State and presented in another from legalisation and similar formalities (including an apostille). It also provides multilingual standard forms as a translation aid. The Regulation applies to a closed list of civil-status facts: birth, being alive, death, name, marriage, parenthood, adoption, domicile or residence, nationality, absence of a criminal record, and similar items. It does not apply to documents issued by a third country, and it does not list company certificates of incorporation, articles of association, or ordinary commercial powers of attorney. It is not a general exemption for EU company documents filed with a Spanish NIF application.

Other international instruments listed by the Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs (including the Athens Convention of 1977, the Vienna Convention of 1976, the London Convention of 1968 and a 1984 exchange of notes with Russia) mainly concern civil-registry or diplomatic and consular documents. They are not a general basis for omitting the apostille on a foreign company’s NIF file.

A document signed before a Spanish notary — for example a power of attorney granted in Spain — is a Spanish public document. It does not need a foreign apostille in order to be used before the Spanish administration.

A special case for certain online sellers appears in a written AEAT information reply obtained by IberTrust (ref. 2026CTE81350003S, 28 October 2026). Where the seller already has a VAT identification number in another EU Member State that can be checked in VIES, and files a certificate from the platform or website confirming that a contract has been signed and that the seller intends to sell in Spain and on other European sites, the AEAT indicated that the Hague apostille or legalisation need not be required for the documents that prove the company exists when applying for the company’s NIF N. In our reading, that exemption does not cover the power of attorney; we still file that document notarised and apostilled. The reply is information under Article 87 of the General Tax Law, not a binding ruling. The conditions and the limits are set out in more detail in our NIF-without-apostille guide.

Official translation: when it is required, and when it can be simplified

The AEAT’s practical guide, citing Law 39/2015 and Article 24(5) of the General Regulation on tax management (RGAT), states that the tax administration may require a translation into Spanish or another official language in Spain when the documents are not in an official language. An official translation is, under Royal Decree 724/2020 and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, one made by a sworn translator or interpreter appointed by that Ministry, or one made or assumed through diplomatic or consular channels, or one recognised as official under EU law.

If the document is already in Spanish, a further translation is not normally needed. Co-official languages in Spain (Catalan, Galician and Basque) may be used before the administration of the relevant Autonomous Community under Article 15 of Law 39/2015. For filings with the AEAT, the safe course is still to file a Spanish translation unless the office has accepted another official language in that file.

The multilingual standard forms under Regulation 2016/1191 are only a translation aid for the civil-status documents covered by that Regulation. They must be issued by the authority of the Member State that issued the public document; they cannot be downloaded and completed by the applicant, and they do not replace the translation of company documents.

The same AEAT written reply also states that, for documents issued in English, French or Italian, an unofficial Spanish translation may be accepted if it is accompanied by a responsible declaration from the person acting as the company’s representative for the census procedure, confirming that the translation corresponds to the full content of the original. We rely on this for the company’s NIF N in the online-seller case described above. We do not assume that it applies automatically to the representative’s NIF M or to the general route. Details and sample wording are in our translation guide.

Selling only online does not, by itself, remove the need for a Spanish NIF

A non-resident or non-established entity must have a NIF when it carries out transactions with tax implications in Spain. “We only sell online” is not, on its own, an exemption.

  • If you register for OSS in Spain (Form 035), you normally need a Spanish NIF first.
  • If you use Union OSS in another Member State, and you have no stock or other taxable presence in Spain, you may not need a Spanish VAT registration solely for those intra-EU distance sales. A Spanish NIF can still be required for other reasons, such as import or a Spanish EPR registration.
  • A marketplace that collects VAT as a deemed supplier on certain B2C sales changes who is the taxable person for those sales. It does not automatically cancel other Spanish obligations that can still require a NIF, including stock in a Spanish warehouse.
  • The AEAT online-seller special case described above simplifies the documents for obtaining the company’s NIF N. It does not waive the NIF. The same reply states that both procedures must be completed: first the representative’s NIF M, then the company’s NIF N.

The basic company application is explained in How a foreign company gets a Spanish NIF; the difference between NIF, VAT, EORI and OSS is in a separate guide.

FAQ

Can I get the apostille in Spain?

Not for a document issued abroad. The apostille is issued by the competent authority of the country where the public document originates. A power of attorney signed before a Spanish notary is a Spanish document and does not need a foreign apostille.

Our company is in the EU. Are our registry documents exempt under Regulation 2016/1191?

Not as a general rule. That Regulation covers a list of civil-status documents, not company incorporation certificates, articles of association or typical commercial powers of attorney.

We sell only through a marketplace that charges VAT. Can we skip the Spanish NIF?

Not automatically. The platform’s role as deemed supplier affects those B2C sales. It does not, by itself, remove other reasons why a Spanish NIF may still be required. If you do need a NIF and you meet the conditions in the AEAT written reply, the company identification documents may be filed without an apostille.

Do English documents always need a sworn translator?

Under the general published rule, the AEAT may require an official translation. In the online-seller case covered by the AEAT written reply, English, French or Italian documents may be filed with an unofficial Spanish translation plus a responsible declaration. That reading is explained, with its limits, in our translation guide.
