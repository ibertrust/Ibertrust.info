Asking Spain’s tax authority a binding question as a non-EU seller: why the DGT sent our consultation back, and the representation both sides must sign

2026-11-13 · IberTrust Global

Spanish tax · written consultations to the DGT · representation · from our own case files, anonymised

Earlier this month we filed a written tax consultation with Spain’s Directorate General of Taxes (Dirección General de Tributos, DGT) for a client: a company established outside the EU that sells online to Spanish consumers and needed a clear answer on which documents it must provide to obtain a Spanish NIF. It already had a non-binding information answer from the tax agency; now it wanted one the authorities must follow.

The consultation went in electronically. A week later a notice arrived through DEHú, the Spanish administration’s electronic notification point. It was not the answer. It was a request to correct a defect, with ten working days to reply. The problem was not the question. It was the representation. The power of attorney we had attached was signed by the client only. The DGT wrote that a power of representation must be signed both by the party granting it and by the representative accepting it, that a company acting as representative must sign electronically, and that anyone signing on behalf of either party must attach the authority that allows them to sign.

Below is what we now check before a consultation is filed for a seller established outside Spain, so that it goes through the first time. Names and reference numbers have been removed.

What a written consultation is, and what it is not

Under Article 88 of the General Tax Law (Ley 58/2003, LGT), taxpayers may ask the tax administration in writing about the tax regime, classification or characterisation that applies to them. The DGT must answer within six months. If it does not, that does not mean it accepts the view set out in the consultation. Under Article 89, the answer is binding on the tax authorities in their dealings with the person who asked, as long as the consultation was filed in time and the facts described have not changed. The authorities must also apply the same criteria to any other taxpayer whose facts are identical.

Who has to be the one asking

The law gives the right to ask to the taxpayer, about the regime that applies to that taxpayer (Article 88(1)). Professional bodies and business associations may ask about questions that affect their members in general (Article 88(3)). For a seller, this means the consultation is filed in the seller’s name and describes the seller’s own facts. The adviser appears as representative, not as the party asking.

Document checklist before filing

  1. The consultation letter, with the minimum content of Article 66(1) of Royal Decree 1065/2007: name and tax number of the taxpayer and, where there is one, of the representative; an express statement of whether any related procedure, appeal or claim is being processed; the question; the background and circumstances, explained clearly; place, date and signature.
  2. Cross-border details, if the question concerns a cross-border transaction or a permanent establishment (Article 66(2)): the group the seller belongs to, if any; a description of the business; the States that may be affected; and persons resident in other States who may be affected.
  3. Proof of representation, signed by both sides. The DGT offers a voluntary standard representation form, valid for a single request, that can be signed electronically or, where appropriate, by hand (Order HAC/1358/2025, Article 2(b)). Its website says representation may also be proved by any other means valid in law. Under Article 1(a) of the same Order, representation and powers registered with the AEAT may be considered sufficient.
  4. Proof of the signatories’ authority. If a director or attorney signs for the seller, or for the representative company, attach the document that gives them that power.
  5. Supporting documents that help the DGT form its view (Article 66(3)), for example a previous information answer.
  6. Electronic filing. Consultations can be filed electronically, and must be when the taxpayer is obliged to deal with the administration by electronic means (Article 66(5)).

If a correction request arrives

  1. Open the notice and keep the acknowledgement of receipt. The period counts from the day after receipt.
  2. Read exactly which defect is listed. In our case it was only the representation.
  3. Collect the missing signatures, from the grantor and from the representative, and the proof of each signatory’s authority.
  4. Reply through the DGT’s electronic registry, using the secure verification code (CSV) printed on the notice, within the period it gives.
  5. Without a reply in time, the consultation is treated as withdrawn and archived (Article 66(6) of Royal Decree 1065/2007; Article 88(4) LGT).

Common mistakes

  • Reusing a power of attorney made for another procedure. In our case, a notarial power signed only by the client and prepared for a tax registration did not satisfy the DGT on its own.
  • No acceptance by the representative. When the representative is a company, its acceptance must carry its electronic signature.
  • Waiting for the answer before meeting a deadline. The consultation does not interrupt any deadline. If an obligation is due, it still has to be met.
  • Filing too late. A consultation filed after the deadline for the obligation it concerns is not admitted (Article 66(7)).
  • Leaving out the statement on pending procedures. It is mandatory, and answers on questions linked to a procedure that had already started are not binding (Article 89(2)).
  • Not watching the electronic mailbox. Our correction request came through DEHú, with a short deadline. Check it regularly while a consultation is open.

Who this does not apply to

  • Customs questions. Article 88(8) LGT leaves consultations on EU customs rules to the Union customs legislation.
  • Non-tax questions, such as packaging or WEEE producer registration with MITECO. The DGT answers on tax regime, classification or characterisation.
  • Questions already tied to an open procedure on the same matter: the answer would not be binding (Article 89(2)).
  • Questions that published doctrine already answers. The DGT itself suggests searching the Ministry of Finance’s tax doctrine database before filing.

FAQ

Is the DGT’s answer binding?

Yes, on the tax authorities in their dealings with the person who asked, provided the consultation was filed in time and the facts have not changed (Article 89(1) LGT). The same criteria apply to other taxpayers with identical facts.

How long does the DGT take to answer?

The law gives it six months from filing. No answer within that time does not mean your view is accepted (Article 88(6) LGT).

Can my adviser file the consultation in its own name?

The right to ask belongs to the taxpayer, about its own tax position. The adviser files as your representative, with a representation signed by both of you.

Does a pending consultation pause my tax deadlines?

No. Deadlines keep running while the DGT studies the question (Article 89(3) LGT).

Can I appeal against the answer?

No. You can appeal against later decisions that apply the answer, not against the answer itself (Article 89(4) LGT).
